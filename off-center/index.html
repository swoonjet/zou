Off Center

The circle designs were one output of four deeper ideas. Pull those ideas loose and they grow cells, mazes, shells, foam, sunflowers, quasicrystals, trees, solids, music and knots just as easily. Fourteen live specimens follow.

Idea 1

Exact moves

Every point comes from earlier points by compass or straightedge. Nothing is placed by eye.

Idea 2

Keep by counting

Choose what survives by counting: circles that overlap, neighbors that touch, generations since the start.

Idea 3

Recipes, not outlines

Store the rule and the steps. The result can be regrown at any size or with one step changed.

Idea 4

Lengths you can build

√2, √3, φ and their friends. The same ratios turn up in shells, paper, music and crystals.

From the vesicaOrganic · cells

Cells that settle into hexagons

The line through a vesica's two points is the perpendicular bisector: everything on one side is nearer one center. Draw it between every pair of seeds and you get Voronoi cells, the pattern of giraffe skin, dragonfly wings and dried mud. Let each seed drift to the middle of its cell and the mess relaxes toward a honeycomb.

Tap the drawing to add a seed.

Keep by counting: red cells have exactly six neighbors. Relaxing raises their share, because six is what flat space prefers.

From corner arcsPattern · meanders

A maze made of quarter circles

Put two quarter arcs in opposite corners of a square, then turn each tile at random. The arcs always connect across tile edges, so they form unbroken rivers and loops: Truchet tiles. Tap a tile to turn it.

Always two colors: color each region by the checkerboard parity of the grid corner inside it, and no two neighbors ever match, however the tiles turn.

From the diagonalGrowth · shells

Spirals from right angles

The diagonal move turns 1 into √2. Repeat it on its own result, always stepping one unit at right angles, and you climb through √3, √4, √5 in a snail shell called the Spiral of Theodorus. Or stack squares on the long side of a rectangle and join their corners with quarter arcs for the golden spiral.

Keep by counting: in the root spiral, red triangles end on whole numbers (√4 = 2, √9 = 3 …). In the golden one, squares follow 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and the rectangle closes in on φ.

From joinSpecial maths · foam

Foam where every bubble touches three

Join was a circle through three points. Ask instead for the circle that touches three circles, and fill every gap with one. The result is an Apollonian gasket: foam that never ends, built by compass at every scale.

Integers inside: set the share to 0.50 and the curvatures (1 ÷ radius) start −1, 2, 2, 3. From then on every bubble's curvature is a whole number, printed inside it.

From the circle, repeatedCurves · envelopes

A heart nobody drew

Draw many circles, each centered on the first circle and passing through one fixed point. None of them is a heart, but together they outline one: the cardioid, the shape of light on the bottom of a coffee cup. Move the point and the family changes.

Outlines without outlines: the curve is an envelope, a shape defined only by what touches it. A point inside gives a dimpled limaçon, a point outside gives one with an inner loop, and touching the axis gives a nephroid.

From the compass aloneSpecial maths · warping

A circle that works as a mirror

Circle inversion swaps inside and outside: a point near the rim stays put, a point near the center flies out to infinity. Lines become circles. Here the flower-of-life lattice is reflected into a lens. Drag the lens around.

Compass only: inversion needs no straightedge. In 1797 Mascheroni showed that every straightedge-and-compass construction can be done with the compass alone, using exactly this trick.

From join, at right anglesOther geometry · hyperbolic

Straight lines in curved space

Inside this disk, the straight lines are arcs that meet the rim at right angles, and the rim is infinitely far away. That is the Poincaré model of hyperbolic geometry, the space of coral, lettuce leaves and Escher's Circle Limit prints. Each arc is a compass construction.

Keep by counting: regions on the inside of an odd number of arcs are red. Seven-fold repeats are not possible as flat tilings, but they are easy here.

From vesica arcsObjects · constant width

Rolling without being round

Take an equilateral triangle and replace each side with an arc centered on the opposite corner. The result is the same width in every direction, so it rolls between two boards that never tilt. Engineers use it for drills that cut square holes, and Britain uses the seven-sided version for its 20p and 50p coins.

Width stays fixed, center doesn't: the red track shows the center bobbing as the shape turns while the top board stays level.

Part two · six more directions, built

Further out

Sunflowers, quasicrystals, trees, solids, music and knots. Each is the same four ideas pointed somewhere new.

From the walkOrganic · phyllotaxis

A walk that never closes

Walking the compass by 60° closes after six steps. Walk instead by 137.5°, the angle that splits a circle in the golden ratio, and let each step move slightly outward. The points never line up, and they pack into the seed spirals of a sunflower, a pinecone or a daisy.

Keep by counting: seeds are colored by their number mod 13, 21 or 34. Those are Fibonacci numbers, and at the golden angle each choice lights up exactly that many spiral arms. Nudge the angle and the spirals snap into straight spokes.

From the pentagon, through φSpecial maths · quasicrystal

Order that never repeats

The pentagon is constructible, so φ is too. Start with a wheel of ten golden triangles and split every triangle by φ, again and again. The result is a Penrose tiling: two rhombus shapes, five-fold symmetry everywhere, and no pattern that repeats by sliding. Real quasicrystals form this way, and their discovery won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Recipe, not outline: the tiling is stored as one rule applied n times. Count the two tile types after each split and their ratio closes in on φ.

From the diagonal and halving, repeatedGrowth · branching

Trees from one rule

Every branch makes two smaller branches at a fixed angle, using a constructible angle and a constructible shrink such as 1/√2 or 1/φ. Run the rule on its own output and you get trees, coral and river deltas. The Pythagoras tree does the same with squares: two squares on the legs of a right triangle, forever.

Keep by counting: the red generation is chosen by its distance from the trunk.

From the vesica, spunOther geometry · solids

Five solids, four numbers

Spin a vesica about its axis and you get two spheres meeting in a circle. The same moves in 3D give the five Platonic solids, and every corner of every one sits at coordinates made only of 0, ±1, ±φ and ±1/φ. The red frames show each recipe: the icosahedron is three golden rectangles at right angles, and the dodecahedron is a cube with roofs. Drag to turn it.

Counting that never fails: corners − edges + faces = 2 for every one of them (Euler's formula).

From measureSound · tuning lattice

A map you can play

Lengths are ratios, and ratios are pitches. Step right by a fifth (3/2) and up-right by a major third (5/4) and the plane fills with a lattice of just intonation. Every small triangle is a chord: pointing up is major, pointing down is minor. Tap a triangle to hear it, a dot to hear one note, or let it wander.

Each step flips one triangle across an edge, keeping two notes and moving one.

The comma: the two red Es are the same note on a piano, but four fifths (81/64) and one third (5/4) miss each other by 21.5 cents. This small gap is why tuning systems exist.

From delete by countingPattern · interlace

Circles that weave

Where two strands cross, one goes over. To make every strand alternate over, under, over, use the same count that deleted regions in the machine: whether a crossing's corner is inside an odd or even number of circles decides who is on top. Any circle construction becomes Celtic-style knotwork.

Why it always works: any drawing of crossing curves can be colored like a checkerboard. Reading that coloring at each crossing gives a consistent over-under everywhere.
